Moving more on dialysis: safe exercise

Gentle ways to build strength, energy and balance, and how to keep your access safe while you do it.

Rahul VarmaRenal Physiotherapist
7 Oct 2026 · 6 min read

Many people on dialysis feel too tired to exercise, yet regular gentle movement is one of the best ways to feel less tired. It builds muscle, helps sleep and mood, steadies blood pressure and blood sugar and lowers the risk of falls. You do not need a gym. You need a plan that suits your body and your dialysis days.

Before you start

Talk to your nephrologist or our physiotherapist first, especially if you have heart problems, poor balance or recent surgery. We will check your blood pressure, your access and your recent results, then suggest a starting point.

  • Fistula or graft arm: keep moving it gently, but do not lift heavy weights or wear tight straps on it.
  • PD catheter: avoid swimming in lakes or pools until your team says it is safe, and keep the exit site covered and dry.
  • Timing: many people feel best exercising on non-dialysis days or during the first half of a session.

Four kinds of movement

Walking

The easiest place to start. Walk at a pace where you can still talk. Begin with 10 minutes and add 2 or 3 minutes each week.

Exercise during dialysis

Our in-chair pedal bikes and leg exercises can be used during the first two hours of a session, with a nurse nearby. Patients often say the time passes faster.

Strength

Sit-to-stand from a chair, wall push-ups and light resistance bands twice a week help you climb stairs and carry shopping more easily.

Balance and stretching

Standing on one foot while holding a chair, heel-to-toe walking and gentle yoga stretches help prevent falls and ease stiffness and cramps.

A sample first month

  1. Week 1: a 10 minute walk on three non-dialysis days.
  2. Week 2: add sit-to-stand exercises, two sets of five.
  3. Week 3: walk 15 minutes and try in-chair pedalling on dialysis days.
  4. Week 4: walk 20 minutes, add a resistance band routine twice a week.

When to stop and rest

Stop if you feel chest pain or tightness, severe breathlessness, dizziness, a racing or irregular heartbeat or sudden cramp. Rest and tell your care team. If chest pain does not settle within a few minutes, call 108.

A little, often, is better than a lot, once in a while.

This article is general information. Ask your own care team before starting a new exercise plan.

Key points

  • Start with 10 minutes and build up slowly.
  • Never put pressure or weights on your fistula arm.
  • Stop and rest if you feel chest pain, dizziness or a racing heart.
