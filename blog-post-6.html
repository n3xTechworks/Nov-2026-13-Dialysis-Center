Living well

Looking after your emotional wellbeing

Low mood and worry are common on dialysis. Here is how to notice them and what really helps.

Lukas BeckerClinical Psychologist
23 Sep 2026 · 6 min read

Starting dialysis changes your week, your diet, your energy and sometimes your work and relationships. It is natural to feel sad, frustrated, anxious or even numb. Up to one in three people on dialysis experience depression at some point. The good news is that emotional health on dialysis can improve, and asking for help is part of good kidney care, not a sign of weakness.

Signs to look out for

  • Feeling low, empty or tearful on most days for two weeks or more.
  • Losing interest in things you used to enjoy.
  • Constant worry, a racing heart or trouble breathing when thinking about treatment.
  • Sleeping much more or much less than usual.
  • Skipping sessions, medicines or meals because it all feels pointless.

Some of these overlap with physical symptoms of kidney disease, such as tiredness and poor sleep. That is why it helps to talk them through with someone who understands both.

What helps

Build a gentle routine

Plan small, enjoyable things on dialysis days and the days in between: a call with a friend, music during your session, a short walk in the evening.

Talk about it

Tell your nurse or doctor how you are feeling. Our renal counsellor offers private sessions in English, Tamil, Hindi and more, at the centre or by video.

Meet others who understand

Our monthly support circle brings together patients at different stages. Many people say it was the first time they felt truly understood.

Look after sleep

Keep regular times, limit tea and coffee after midday and tell us about itching or restless legs, which are often treatable.

You are more than your treatment schedule. Keeping the things that make you you is part of the plan.

For families and carers

Caring for someone on dialysis can be tiring and lonely. Carers are welcome at our support circle, and our social worker can help with practical matters such as transport, leave from work and government schemes.

If you need help now

If you feel unsafe or have thoughts of harming yourself, please call 108 or go to the nearest emergency department. You can also call the national Tele-MANAS mental health helpline on 14416 at any time.

This article is general information and does not replace care from a qualified professional.

Key points

  • Feeling low, worried or angry is common and treatable.
  • Routine, sleep and talking to others all help.
  • Carers need support too; they are welcome at our groups.
